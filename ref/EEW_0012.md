#### Abstract

Rapid and accurate estimation of earthquake moment magnitude is crucial for early warning systems, particularly for alerting coastal populations vulnerable to tsunamigenic hazards. Most seismic-based estimation approaches introduce time delays that limit applicability near the source, while geodetic approaches have been limited to empirical scaling relationships. We extend a physics-based approach for seismogeodetic moment magnitude (Mwg) estimation initially developed for thrust earthquakes to also include strike-slip and normal fault mechanisms by examining seventeen Mw 7.0 to 9.1 earthquakes. We find that considering S-wave propagation is critical for accurately estimating the magnitude of strike-slip events. Radiation pattern (RP) corrections offer substantial benefits for normal fault events but are difficult to compute in real-time. However, RP corrections can be neglected for strike-slip and thrust events, allowing accurate Mwg estimates within about 2-3 minutes of earthquake initiation in early warning scenarios. We further broaden the seismogeodetic approach by interpolating coseismic windows from collocated GNSS and/or accelerometer stations to stand-alone GNSS stations, thereby increasing the size and geometry of the available network. We present an integrated workflow for rapid Mwg estimation, which leverages tectonic information from subduction zone geometry to inform focal mechanism selection, instead of using uncertain hypocentral depths. Overall, our extended approach provides rapid earthquake magnitudes (˜2-3 minutes after earthquake initiation) for moderate to large events (Mw > 7) with an Mwg accuracy of 0.2 magnitude units. Our approach is useful for operational environments where shortened warning times are essential.

### **Abstract**

Most seismic-based estimation approaches introduce time delays that limit applicability near the source, while geodetic approaches have been limited to empirical scaling relationships. We extend a physics-based approach for seismogeodetic moment magnitude (*Mwg*) estimation initially developed for thrust earthquakes to also include strike-slip and normal fault mechanisms by examining seventeen *Mw* 7.0 to 9.1 earthquakes. However, RP corrections can be neglected for strike-slip and thrust events, allowing accurate *Mwg* estimates within about 2-3 minutes of earthquake initiation in early warning scenarios. Our approach is useful for operational environments where shortened warning times are essential.

### **Plain Language Summary**

 Earthquakes that occur near the coast can generate damaging ground motions and tsunamis within minutes, leaving very little time to issue warnings. Current warning systems often rely on seismic data, which can take several minutes to provide reliable estimates of earthquake size, or on geodetic data, which relies on empirical relationships. In this study, we extend and improve a physics-based method that rapidly and accurately estimates the size of large earthquakes using both ground acceleration and GNSS displacement data. Our approach can determine earthquake magnitude within about 2–3 minutes after initiation, regardless of the faulting mechanism. This capability enhances earthquake and tsunami early warning systems by providing fast, physics- based magnitude estimates with an accuracy of about ±0.2 magnitude units compared to reported values. This method can be applied in tsunami warning centers and emergency response systems to issue earlier and more reliable alerts, giving coastal communities more time to take protective action.

### **1 Introduction**

 A timely early warning provides information about the expected tsunami hazard area, enabling the evacuation of populations at risk before the first tsunami waves arrive. In the case of an earthquake-induced tsunami, this area is determined by the earthquake's moment magnitude (*Mw*), focal mechanism at the centroid (centroid moment tensor - CMT), and fault slip distribution. Rapid estimation of these parameters is crucial for accurately assessing tsunami hazards and risks (Melgar & Bock, 2015; Melgar et al., 2016). Traditionally, tsunami early  warnings rely on broadband P-wave moment magnitude (*Mwp*) (Tsuboi et al., 1995, 2002) from seismic stations with epicentral distances of > ~5-10°. While sufficient for populations beyond ~3-5 minutes tsunami travel time from the earthquake source, the delays associated with *Mwp* estimation times are inadequate for coastal populations residing closer to large (*Mw* > 8) and complex events or tsunami earthquakes characterized by slow rupture velocities.

 Efforts to improve the timeliness of accurate source parameter estimates have led to the development of the W-phase CMT (WCMT) inversion for *Mw* (Kanamori & Rivera, 2008), which typically yields results within 5–20 minutes of earthquake origin time (Rivera & Kanamori, 2009) using regional to teleseismic broadband data. Thus, accurate W-phase *Mw* and CMT estimates are not available early enough to warn the closest coastal populations (<~3-5 degrees) to the earthquake source, where most tsunami-related casualties occur. Accelerometers provide unclipped seismic data closer to the earthquake source than broadband velocity sensors. However, the accelerometer data need to be integrated twice to obtain the displacements required for source parameter determination, which can cause time series drift and misalignment (Boore, 2001; Melgar et al., 2013).

 Recent advances in real-time seismology have led to the development of ShakeAlert, a multi-algorithm earthquake early warning system for the US West Coast (Kohler et al., 2020). ShakeAlert uses two algorithms for real-time magnitude estimation. The EPIC (Earthquake Point- source Integrated Code) algorithm estimates magnitude and location by fitting a point-source model to seismic data from strong-motion and broadband stations. EPIC is designed for rapid magnitude determination in the early stages of rupture and is suitable for small to moderate events (M < ∼7). In contrast, FinDer (Finite Fault Detector) computes magnitude by mapping the evolving spatial distribution of peak ground motions (e.g., peak ground acceleration, PGA, or peak ground velocity, PGV) and comparing the motions to empirically derived templates of finite fault ruptures. While FinDer effectively avoids magnitude saturation, extending the magnitude range to M 9.0+ and capturing rupture growth, the method remains fundamentally empirical, relying on precomputed ground motion patterns rather than physical modeling of the source.

 To address these limitations, GNSS-based approaches have emerged as powerful alternatives. Real-time GNSS networks can measure both static and dynamic displacements with high temporal resolution (1–10 Hz) and without clipping, offering an effective means to capture permanent offsets in the near-field (Bock et al., 2000; Nikolaidis et al., 2001; Wright et al., 2012). GNSS-based peak ground displacements have been used to quickly estimate earthquake magnitude through empirical scaling relationships (Crowell et al., 2013; Melgar et al., 2015), with successful applications in early warning systems across various regions. These approaches, now integrated into platforms like G-FAST (Crowell et al., 2018) in the Western U.S and REGARD in Japan (Kawamoto et al., 2016), also provide real-time centroid moment tensor and fault slip estimates (e.g., Crowell et al., 2018; Melgar et al., 2012).

 Rapid magnitude estimation algorithms require a seismic trigger, and GNSS instruments cannot detect P-waves. To circumvent this problem, data from collocated accelerometer and GNSS stations are used to generate unclipped broadband displacement and velocity waveforms that capture dynamic, earthquake-induced shaking and static offsets (Bock et al., 2011; Smyth & Wu, 2007). These broadband displacements and velocities (hereafter referred to as seismogeodetic data) can be used to provide rapid seismogeodetic earthquake magnitude (*Mwg)* estimates for thrust earthquakes (Golriz et al., 2023) (hereafter referred to as Golriz2023), within  2–3 minutes of earthquake origin time, using the vertical component of seismogeodetic displacement as an approximate source time function. However, normal and strike-slip events remain challenging due to different wavefield characteristics and radiation patterns. Golriz2023 used collocated (GNSS and acceleration) stations and far-field P-wave energy generated by a double-couple point source in a homogeneous elastic medium without accounting for seismic wave radiation pattern effects on each source to site path. Instead, they used a constant representing the average P-wave radiation pattern over the focal sphere (Tsuboi et al., 1995). [Our magnitude notation should not be confused with the Das Magnitude scale *Mwg* introduced by (Das et al., 2019) to address the limitations of the conventional *Mw* for magnitudes below 7.5].

 Accounting for radiation patterns in real-time is complex and requires knowledge of earthquake source parameters (strike, dip, rake), which can be obtained through a near real-time CMT solution. Although several algorithms have been developed for this purpose (e.g., W-phase; G-FAST), limitations remain. Inadequate azimuthal coverage can lead to ambiguous or unstable solutions (Duputel et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2017). Epicentral and centroid misalignment can introduce significant errors in fault orientation estimation, affecting ground motion and tsunami predictions(Crowell et al., 2018). Earthquake source depth estimation biases of up to 10 km have been observed, impacting the accuracy of radiation pattern calculations. Additionally, real-time CMT inversions are sensitive to data dropouts and latency, though G-FAST is robust against moderate errors. Lastly, the inclusion of grid search techniques for improved fault plane geometry estimation increases computational demands and is not yet feasible in a real-time environment.

 In this study, we expand on the work of Golriz et al. (2023), based only on the far-field P- wave term in Equation 1 of Madariaga et al. (2019), by adding the far-field S-wave and the intermediate-field P-wave and S-wave terms. We also account for all radiation pattern terms and use the radial component of the horizontal motion instead of the vertical component. Our new approach improves our ability to obtain accurate *Mwg* estimates for normal and strike slip fault events known to produce tsunamis (e.g., the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point, Alaska and the *Mw* 7.5 Palu, Indonesia strike-slip earthquakes, and the *Mw* 8.2 Chiapas, Mexico and the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands, Alaska normal earthquakes) and also extends its application to local earthquake early warning. We apply our approach to seventeen earthquakes, including the eight thrust events, and the single normal event, from Golriz et al. (2023), six strike-slip events, and an additional normal event. We also use the available seismogeodetic and/or accelerometer data to estimate P-wave arrival times at locations where only GNSS data are available, thus enhancing the usable network of GNSS stations.

 Our study is motivated by the goal of developing an operational seismogeodesy-based warning system for local tsunamis and earthquakes. To demonstrate real-world applicability, we leverage the operational capabilities of the NOAA/NWS/Pacific Tsunami Warning Center (PTWC), which provides a model for rapid tsunami warnings. PTWC performs real-time data reception, earthquake detection, location, and magnitude estimation, partially within the Earthworm seismic network environment (Hirshorn et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 1995)

### **2.2 Data Analysis**

 We gathered collocated GNSS and accelerometer data recorded during nine earthquakes with magnitudes in the range of 7.0 < *Mw* < 8.2. Following the practice of Golriz2023, we define a collocation as a GNSS-accelerometer pair separated by no more than 5 km. Seven of these earthquakes were caused by strike-slip faulting in various tectonic environments: North American and Eurasian plate boundary (*Mw* 7.0 Ridgecrest, California and *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor- Cucapah, Mexico), Arabian and Eurasian plate boundary (*Mw* 7.8 Pazarcik and *Mw* 7.7 Elbistan, Turkey), Mendocino triple junction where the Pacific, North America, and Juan de Fuca/Gorda plates meet (*Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino, California), and the subduction zone interface between the Pacific and North America plates (*Mw* 7.6 and Mw 7.3 Sand Point, Alaska). The other two earthquakes are normal faulting events in the Pacific Basin, offshore Mexico (*Mw* 8.2, Chiapas) and Alaska (*Mw* 7.9, Rat Islands) (Table 1a). For the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands earthquake, focal mechanism solutions reported by the GCMT catalog cannot differentiate between rupture on a near-vertical fault or a shallowly dipping fault (Motazedian & Ma, 2022). Since the reported focal mechanism involves oblique normal faulting, we represent the radiation pattern for this event using a shallow dipping fault (see Table 1), which aligns with other studies (e.g., Miyazawa, 2015; Ye et al., 2014).

 We also test our expanded approach on the dataset of eight thrust earthquakes analyzed by Golriz et al. (2023) (Table 1b). All high-rate (1-5 Hz) GNSS data are processed using the precise point positioning (PPP) software PRIDE-PPP-AR (Geng et al., 2019). Accelerometer data sampled at 50-200 Hz is obtained from EarthScope [\(https://ds.iris.edu/ds/\)](https://ds.iris.edu/ds/). We use a Kalman filter with an acceleration multiplier of 10 to combine the GNSS and accelerometer data into broadband, 500-200 Hz sample rate displacement and velocity seismogeodetic time series (Bock et al., 2011; Golriz et al., 2023; Saunders et al., 2016).

 To accurately compute the seismogeodetic magnitude during the coseismic window while excluding postseismic deformation, we follow the approach of Golriz et al. (2021), which uses seismogeodetic velocity data to define the coseismic time window by integrating the normalized velocity squared until 99%-point convergence.

### **3.2 Incorporating Stand-Alone GNSS Stations**

 Currently, accelerometers are collocated with only a small number of the total available GNSS stations, thus limiting the widespread applicability of the seismogeodetic approach. Furthermore, GNSS instruments alone are not sensitive enough to detect earthquake P-waves  and determine the width of the coseismic window (Golriz et al., 2021). We overcome these limitations by interpolating the P-wave first arrival times from nearby accelerometers(collocated or stand-alone). We use P-wave arrival times directly from a travel-time curve for those arrival times that fall far enough away from their predicted arrival times (a difference of more than 15 seconds). Once a coseismic window is determined from an accelerometer, its size can be interpolated to the stand-alone GNSS stations, providing additional information that helps constrain the earthquake magnitude and associated uncertainty, especially when the collocated station network is sparse.

 We interpolate the coseismic window computed at collocated stations and/or stations instrumented only with accelerometers to the stand-alone GNSS stations using Delaunay triangulation from four closest neighbors. We apply the coseismic interpolation from accelerometer-only stations for events with fewer than five collocated stations. If a window cannot be interpolated due to network sparsity, we use the coseismic window of the nearest neighbor within 10 km. For the accelerometers, we first integrate the data into velocities and apply a high-pass filter to ensure consistency with the computation of the coseismic window using seismogeodetic velocities. We then use our algorithm to compute *Mwg* over the interpolated window, expanding the usable network. We perform the interpolation with and without applying the radiation pattern corrections. However, for the stand-alone GNSS stations we focus on the results when we do not apply the radiation pattern corrections as we also do for the seismogeodetic (collocated) station analysis to mimic a real-time implementation. We find that in most cases 12 estimates from stand-alone stations remain within ±0.2 magnitude units of the collocated estimates, underscoring the robustness of the interpolation method. Supplementing the available network with stand-alone GNSS data nearly doubles the number of stations for the *Mw* 7.0 Ridgecrest and *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor-Cucapah earthquakes. [We do not have any stand-alone GNSS stations for the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Island earthquake.] We summarize our results in Table 3 (the results for the coseismic window interpolation with the radiation patterns applied are summarized in Table S2).

### **3.3 Performance on Thrust Earthquakes**

 We evaluate our new algorithm on the eight thrust earthquakes from Golriz et al. (2023) (Table 1b) and provide a summary comparison in Table 4. We find that the intermediate term is not required for optimal performance (Equation 8; Cint = 0) as its inclusion leads to systematic underestimations of *Mwg* for most events (Table S3). As seen in Figure 6 and Table 4, the algorithm's performance on thrust earthquakes is comparable to the results obtained by the Golriz2023 method, when radiation patterns (RP) are included. Without the RP corrections, we observe a significant and persistent underestimate of up to 0.4 magnitude units compared to Golriz2023 and the GCMT *Mw* estimates, particularly for the larger events (*Mw* 8.8 Maule and *Mw* 9.1 Tokachi-oki earthquakes).

 Interestingly, for the GCMT *Mw* 6.9 Kilauea, Hawaii earthquake, the two versions of the new method (with and without RP corrections) yield very similar *Mwg* estimates: 7.28 ± 0.14 with RP corrections and 7.18 ± 0.12 without them (Table 4). Both are closer to the higher *Mw* 7.2 estimates reported by Bai et al. (2018) and Lay et al. (2018), which have been linked to the inverse relationship between dip and seismic moment for shallow depth faults. We attribute the minimal difference in *Mwg* estimates to the configuration of the stations, which are approximately aligned along a single value of (the horizontal angle between the slip direction and the station) of 180° (Figure S2) and are sufficiently distant that the zenith angle is about 90∘ 395 . This station configuration results in the far-field S-wave RP coefficient /. 396 equal to 1 (Equation 4), similar to the assumption made when RP corrections are not applied. Thus, the variation in the estimates arises only from the far-field P-wave RP coefficient, AFP. However, because AFP 398 is less dominant than AFS 399 due to the higher P-wave velocity, it has a negligible effect on the far-field coefficient (Equation 7), resulting in the small difference between the *Mwg* estimates with and without RP corrections.

### **4 Discussion**

### **4.1 Performance Across Faulting Styles**

 We demonstrate the performance of our algorithm on a dataset of seventeen earthquakes comprising nine moderate to large magnitude strike-slip and normal fault earthquakes, as well as the eight thrust earthquake dataset from Golriz et al. (2023), representing diverse faulting mechanisms and tectonic settings. The improved algorithm provides an accurate seismogeodetic moment magnitude estimate within 0.2 units of the "true" GCMT reported magnitude for most strike-slip earthquakes, without radiation pattern corrections (Table 1). Our method is suitable for real-time implementation, even when relying on a limited number of collocated GNSS and accelerometer stations. Using the horizontal displacement component, along with the intermediate term of the double couple representation of the displacement field (Equation 1), proves essential for accurate magnitude estimation in strike-slip and normal faulting environments. The use of the intermediate term reduces the difference between *Mwg*  and *Mw* (Figure S3) for two strike-slip earthquakes (*Mw* 7.0, Ridgecrest, California, and the *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor-Cucapah, Mexico), where both network density and geometry are favorable. The radial component exhibits a stronger signal with a higher signal-to-noise ratio than the vertical  component analyzed by Golriz et al. (2023) and has proven to be more effective in these settings at longer distances, with significantly improved *Mwg* estimates.

 Our physics-based approach does not rely on statistical analysis of historical earthquakes, thus accounting for event specific characteristics. The empirical PGD-based magnitude estimates (e.g., Ruhl et al., 2018) are inconsistent in their performance when applied to events outside the dataset used to derive their scaling constants. For example, the empirical PGD-based magnitude generally underestimates the magnitude for thrust earthquakes (Golriz et al., 2023), largely underestimates the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point and the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands, Alaska earthquakes, and largely overestimates the *Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino, California earthquake. These three earthquakes are considered more complex and thus are expected to deviate from non-ergodic empirically based relationships that do not account for event specific source, path, and site effects.

 The accuracy of our *Mwg* approximations to moment magnitude vary as a function of station density and coverage. For events with high station coverage, such as the *Mw* 7.0 Ridgecrest and *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor-Cucapah earthquakes, our *Mwg* estimates (without radiation pattern corrections) are within 0.05 magnitude units of the GCMT *Mw* values, in contrast to the rest of the earthquake dataset, suggesting that station coverage plays a significant role in estimating *Mwg*. For example, despite having the same GCMT moment magnitude and similar depth as the Ridgecrest event (Table 1a), the offshore *Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino earthquake has sparse near-source and azimuthal seismogeodetic coverage and we overestimate its magnitude with (12 = 7.76 ± 0.70) and without (12 = 7.39 ± 0.29) radiation pattern correction (Figure 3). This magnitude overestimation, particularly in the case without radiation pattern corrections, can be attributed to the azimuthal station coverage of the event. Most stations are positioned approximately perpendicular to the fault slip direction, which is expected to receive less energy in the radial component of the horizontal motion used in this study (Equation 4). By assigning the radiation pattern coefficients a value of 1, as done in the case without radiation pattern corrections, rather than a value closer to zero that better reflects this station configuration, we overestimate the *Mwg* (Equations 6-8).

 For the *Mw* 7.8 Pazarcik and *Mw* 7.7 Elbistan earthquakes, we tested both 1 Hz and 5 Hz GNSS displacement data. Higher-rate sampling can improve resolution of dynamic ground motion, reduce aliasing (Smalley, 2009), and improve accuracy, particularly when integrated with high-frequency acceleration data, as in this study. In our limited test cases, *Mwg* estimates were roughly similar for both rates (Table 2). For Pazarcik, the *Mwg* estimates are 7.61 using 1 Hz and 7.68 using 5 Hz data (1 station); for Elbistan, the *Mwg* estimates are 7.49 ± 0.09 from 1 Hz data (2 stations) and 7.56 ± 0.08 from 5 Hz data (4 stations). While the 5 Hz results suggest slight improvements, the dataset is too limited to draw strong conclusions. However, our findings are consistent with a similar analysis performed on 1 and 5 Hz GNSS data from the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquake (Melgar et al., 2019). Additionally, 5 Hz data requiressignificantly more storage space and stresses the real-time data flow, which may impact its practical use. Further analysis using more stations and events is needed to evaluate the utility of 5 Hz data for real-time applications.

# **4.2 Sensitivity to Radiation Pattern Corrections**

 We find that correcting for radiation patterns improves our *Mwg* estimates of moment magnitude, especially for normal and thrust faulting events, where the *Mwg* deviates the most from *Mw* if radiation pattern corrections are omitted. However, in the Golriz2023 approach, radiation pattern corrections are not required for thrust earthquakes. In contrast, for normal- fault earthquakes (e.g., Chiapas and Rat Islands)**,** neglecting the radiation pattern leads to systematic underestimation or overestimation, indicating that its inclusion is indispensable for both approaches to such geometries.

 For strike-slip events, our results show that radiation pattern corrections have minimal influence on *Mwg*, suggesting that they can be omitted in real-time applications where source parameters are uncertain. This is further supported by our results for the 2019 Ridgecrest and 2010 El Mayor-Cucapah events, where the inclusion or exclusion of radiation pattern corrections yielded nearly identical *Mwg* values. However, there are two exceptions. Analysis of the *Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino earthquake yielded a significantly improved estimate of *Mwg* 7.39 ± 0.29 without radiation pattern corrections, compared to *Mwg* 7.76 ± 0.70 when using radiation pattern corrections. This might be a result of incorrectly reported source parameters, as this event is considered very complex and has not yet been studied in detail. Another deviation is the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point earthquake with a very low no-radiation pattern corrections estimate of 7.33 ± 0.04, compared to 7.63 ± 0.12 with radiation pattern corrections, which we attribute to the complexity of a two-stage rupture process (Wan et al., 2022). Despite having a strike-slip faulting mechanism, the moderate dip of the fault (49°; see Table 1) is not typical of strike-slip ruptures. In contrast, for the 2025 *Mw* 7.3 Sand Point earthquake, which occurred in nearly the same location and had a similar dip (57°), our algorithm performed well, yielding a seismogeodetic magnitude of 7.21 ± 0.16. This difference may be related to variations in station configuration between the two events (Figure 2) or to additional rupture complexity associated with the 2020 event.

### **4.3 Relative Performance When Including Stand-Alone GNSS Station**

 Stand-alone GNSS stations yield *Mwg* estimates generally in agreement with those from collocated sensors for strike-slip and normal earthquakes (Table 3; and Figures 4 and 5). Our use of Delaunay triangulation or nearest-neighbor interpolation helps overcome limitations in collocated station coverage for regions such as Turkey and Alaska. For example, for the *Mw* 7.8 Pazarcik earthquake, we added four stand-alone GNSS stations, which allowed us to quantify magnitude uncertainty. Similarly, for the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point earthquake, three additional stand- alone stations improved the magnitude estimate and yielded a significantly more reliable uncertainty range. For the *Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino earthquake, we analyzed sixteen stand-alone GNSS stations at epicentral distances < 200 km (all collocated stations were located at distances > 200 km). Interpolation was successful due to the regional network density. This closer-to-source stand-alone GNSS network improves the magnitude estimate from 7.39 ± 0.29 to 7.24 ± 0.20. In this case, incorporating accelerometers to compute and interpolate the coseismic windows to the stand-alone GNSS network added two additional stations (13 in total) and resulted in a slightly higher *Mwg* estimate of 7.30 ± 0.23 (Figure S4). This suggests that using coseismic  windows interpolated from accelerometers may be less critical in regions with sufficient seismogeodetic coverage, such as California.

 Since stand-alone GNSS sites are usually closer to the epicenter, they perform similarly to collocated seismogeodetic stations located farther away due to a high signal-to-noise ratio in displacement. However, when only far-field stand-alone GNSS data is available, seismogeodetic networks are preferable due to their higher signal quality and high signal-to-noise ratio compared to data from stand-alone GNSS sites. Additionally, the coseismic window is explicitly calculated from the seismogeodetic data, whereas for stand-alone GNSS stations, it must be interpolated, introducing additional uncertainty into the magnitude estimation process.

### **4.4 Real-time Implementation Consideration**

 We compare the median seismogeodetic moment magnitude (*Mwg*) evolution over time for all earthquakes analyzed in this study with the results obtained by Golriz2023 (Figure 7). In general, our new approach converges to a stable *Mwg* estimate within less than two hundred seconds after origin time, when we apply radiation pattern corrections (orange lines). This rapid convergence includes large events such as the *Mw* 9.1 Tohoku-oki, Japan, and the *Mw* 8.8 Maule, Chile earthquakes. However, real-time implementation of this algorithm remains challenging, as a rapid and stable centroid moment tensor solution and the derived source parameters are not typically available during the first few minutes after an earthquake. For strike-slip mechanism earthquakes, our *Mwg* estimates converge to the GCMT *Mw* even without radiation pattern corrections (green lines), making this algorithm suitable for real-time implementation. In contrast, for thrust and normal faults (Table 1), our new algorithm significantly underestimates *Mw* if we do not apply radiation pattern corrections.

 Golriz et al. (2023) used only vertical components, which exhibit higher noise relative to the horizontal components, and while the Golriz et al. (2023) approach performs well for thrust earthquakes, it largely overestimates the magnitudes of strike-slip and normal events below *Mw* 7.6 (Figure 1) because of the elevated vertical noise contamination.

 The comparisons in Figure 7 illustrate that the faulting mechanism should inform the choice of which method to use to rapidly estimate *Mwg* in a real-time operational setting, when no radiation patterns are considered. In thrust environments, the method presented in Golriz2023 is preferred as it does not require radiation pattern corrections. Similarly, our new method is preferred for strike-slip earthquakes where radiation corrections are not required. One way to identify thrust events is to determine if the epicenter is above an offshore subduction zone ( e.g., using Slab2; Hayes et al., 2018). If the epicenter is located above a subduction zone, we can assume that the hypocenter lies on the slab and that it is a thrust event. This assumption becomes more likely as the moment magnitude exceeds about *Mw* 8.

 Therefore, we propose the following strategy: 1) determine a rapid preliminary earthquake epicenter location using local-to-regional phase associators (e.g., the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center uses Earthworm picker and phase associator modules); 2) If the event occurs over a subduction zone based on a rapid query of the Slab2 subduction zone model (Hayes et al., 2018), use the hypocentral depth to the slab and calculate *Mwg* using Golriz2023. If the earthquake does not occur over a subduction zone, classify it as a strike-slip event and apply the new algorithm without radiation pattern corrections (Figure 8).

 Slab2 tends to closely match the geometry of thrust earthquakes reported in the GCMT catalog and can help distinguish between different faulting mechanisms (Tables 5, S4). Although Slab2 can be used to differentiate between normal and thrust ruptures, it is specifically designed to represent subduction zones and does not capture normal fault geometries (see Tables 5, S4, e.g., the *Mw* 8.2 Chiapas, Mexico, and *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands events). However, a deeper event (i.e., beneath the top of the slab) might indicate a normal fault mechanism that can also aid in selecting  the appropriate algorithm (e.g., the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands event). In such cases, the Golriz2023 method is preferable for normal events, despite an overestimation of about 0.3 magnitude units, as our new algorithm without radiation pattern corrections underestimates by a similar amount (Table 2).

 We have demonstrated that using Slab2 in our workflow can help determine the hypocenter depth, which is typically more challenging to identify in real-time. We have also shown that our *Mwg* estimate is insensitive to the difference between the reported and Slab2- inferred hypocentral depths, which are mostly within 10 km (Table 5), resulting in nearly identical *Mwg* estimates. The interpolation of coseismic windows to stand-alone GNSS stations expands the applicability of the seismogeodetic method beyond the limited set of collocated GNSS and seismic stations. We highlight the importance of using all available seismic and GNSS data in real- time applications to improve the robustness of *Mwg* estimates. However, complex rupture geometries (e.g., multi-fault or cascading ruptures) or more isolated regions with limited instrumentation may render such interpolations unreliable. A quantification of the interpolation error (e.g., bias in window start time) would be valuable in future implementations.

 This study also identifies multiple limitations that pose challenges for real-time implementation. We show that for most of the analyzed events, the number of stations is small (≤ 5), and the azimuthal coverage is limited. For example, for the *Mw* 7.8 Pazarcik and the *Mw* 7.7 Elbistan earthquakes in Turkey, we have only one and two collocated stations, respectively. Although we underestimate the magnitude of both events, we use *Mw* estimates reported by the  GCMT, which provides an upper bound estimate for the Elbistan earthquake, ranging from *Mw* 7.5 (USGS) to *Mw* 7.7. This shows that our results are within their uncertainties. However, rupture complexity related to the unusual source fault dip of 46° (Table 1) for a strike-slip earthquake might also affect our estimate. While using radiation patterns may remedy this problem, as demonstrated for the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point earthquake, the approach requires source parameters (e.g., strike, dip, rake) that are not available in real-time. However, future developments may enable more rapid source parameter estimation. In the absence of such information, algorithms that rely on average behavior without radiation pattern corrections will struggle to account for event-specific source complexity. Thus, for events with good instrumental coverage and sufficient data, such as those strike-slip earthquakes in California (e.g., *Mw* 7.0 Ridgecrest and *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor-Cucapah), the seismogeodetic method performs well. Our study motivates the continued deployment of high-rate GNSS stations with real-time data transmission and collocation with accelerometers to enhance GNSS-based early warning systems and to mitigate seismic and tsunami hazards.

 Another limitation of our hybrid approach is the confusion of strike-slip earthquakes with a thrust mechanism on subduction interfaces; this will trigger the Golriz et al. (2023) method for thrust earthquakes in real-time implementation. This is the case for the *Mw* 7.3 and *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point earthquakes. While Golriz2023 overestimated *Mwg* for both events, the *Mw* 7.6 estimate (7.83 ± 0.12) was within a reasonable error of 0.25 magnitude units, whereas the *Mw* 7.3 event was substantially overestimated (*Mwg* = 7.97 ± 0.35) (Table 2). Such cases may complicate the real-time assessment of similar events. However, earthquakes bigger than ~*Mw* 8 above offshore subduction zones are most likely interplate subduction zone thrust events.

# **5 Conclusions**

 This study presents a hybrid approach to seismogeodetic magnitude (*Mwg*) estimation, which builds on the framework established by Golriz et al. (2023) for thrust earthquakes. We enhance the original method by adding terms for far-field S-waves, intermediate-field P- and S-waves, and source radiation. We also expand spatial coverage by interpolating P-wave arrival times and coseismic end times from collocated seismogeodetic and/or stand-alone accelerometers to stand-alone GNSS stations. This hybrid solution combines both intermediate-field and far-field formulations, each tailored to different physical mechanisms, resulting in more accurate *Mwg* estimates. The new approach performs particularly well for strike-slip earthquakes. Importantly, the method is suitable for real-time implementation. While the complete algorithm includes radiation pattern corrections for maximum accuracy, we also demonstrate that a simplified version offers a viable and efficient solution for operational use. When integrated with tectonic context information derived from Slab2, this implementation provides a viable and efficient solution in both local/regional and teleseismic earthquake and tsunami early warning systems. Including radiation pattern corrections improves our *Mwg* estimates for strike-slip, normal, and thrust mechanisms. These corrections can be applied as real-time source characterization capabilities evolve, ensuring that the seismogeodetic approach presented here is both forward compatible and operationally relevant.

# **Abstract**

 Rapid and accurate estimation of earthquake moment magnitude is crucial for early warning systems, particularly for alerting coastal populations vulnerable to tsunamigenic hazards. Most seismic-based estimation approaches introduce time delays that limit applicability near the source, while geodetic approaches have been limited to empirical scaling relationships. We extend a physics-based approach for seismogeodetic moment magnitude (*Mwg*) estimation initially developed for thrust earthquakes to also include strike-slip and normal fault mechanisms by examining seventeen *Mw* 7.0 to 9.1 earthquakes. We find that considering S-wave propagation is critical for accurately estimating the magnitude of strike-slip events. Radiation pattern (RP) corrections offer substantial benefits for normal fault events but are difficult to compute in real- time. However, RP corrections can be neglected for strike-slip and thrust events, allowing accurate *Mwg* estimates within about 2-3 minutes of earthquake initiation in early warning scenarios. We further broaden the seismogeodetic approach by interpolating coseismic windows from collocated GNSS and/or accelerometer stations to stand-alone GNSS stations, thereby increasing the size and geometry of the available network. We present an integrated workflow for rapid *Mwg* estimation, which leverages tectonic information from subduction zone geometry to inform focal mechanism selection, instead of using uncertain hypocentral depths. Overall, our extended approach provides rapid earthquake magnitudes (~2-3 minutes after earthquake initiation) for moderate to large events (*Mw* > 7) with an *Mwg* accuracy of ±0.2 magnitude units. Our approach is useful for operational environments where shortened warning times are essential.

### **Plain Language Summary**

 Earthquakes that occur near the coast can generate damaging ground motions and tsunamis within minutes, leaving very little time to issue warnings. Current warning systems often rely on seismic data, which can take several minutes to provide reliable estimates of earthquake size, or on geodetic data, which relies on empirical relationships. In this study, we extend and improve a physics-based method that rapidly and accurately estimates the size of large earthquakes using both ground acceleration and GNSS displacement data. Our approach can determine earthquake magnitude within about 2–3 minutes after initiation, regardless of the faulting mechanism. This capability enhances earthquake and tsunami early warning systems by providing fast, physics- based magnitude estimates with an accuracy of about ±0.2 magnitude units compared to reported values. This method can be applied in tsunami warning centers and emergency response systems to issue earlier and more reliable alerts, giving coastal communities more time to take protective action.

### **1 Introduction**

 A timely early warning provides information about the expected tsunami hazard area, enabling the evacuation of populations at risk before the first tsunami waves arrive. In the case of an earthquake-induced tsunami, this area is determined by the earthquake's moment magnitude (*Mw*), focal mechanism at the centroid (centroid moment tensor - CMT), and fault slip distribution. Rapid estimation of these parameters is crucial for accurately assessing tsunami hazards and risks (Melgar & Bock, 2015; Melgar et al., 2016). Traditionally, tsunami early  warnings rely on broadband P-wave moment magnitude (*Mwp*) (Tsuboi et al., 1995, 2002) from seismic stations with epicentral distances of > ~5-10°. While sufficient for populations beyond ~3-5 minutes tsunami travel time from the earthquake source, the delays associated with *Mwp* estimation times are inadequate for coastal populations residing closer to large (*Mw* > 8) and complex events or tsunami earthquakes characterized by slow rupture velocities.

 Efforts to improve the timeliness of accurate source parameter estimates have led to the development of the W-phase CMT (WCMT) inversion for *Mw* (Kanamori & Rivera, 2008), which typically yields results within 5–20 minutes of earthquake origin time (Rivera & Kanamori, 2009) using regional to teleseismic broadband data. Thus, accurate W-phase *Mw* and CMT estimates are not available early enough to warn the closest coastal populations (<~3-5 degrees) to the earthquake source, where most tsunami-related casualties occur. Accelerometers provide unclipped seismic data closer to the earthquake source than broadband velocity sensors. However, the accelerometer data need to be integrated twice to obtain the displacements required for source parameter determination, which can cause time series drift and misalignment (Boore, 2001; Melgar et al., 2013).

 Recent advances in real-time seismology have led to the development of ShakeAlert, a multi-algorithm earthquake early warning system for the US West Coast (Kohler et al., 2020). ShakeAlert uses two algorithms for real-time magnitude estimation. The EPIC (Earthquake Point- source Integrated Code) algorithm estimates magnitude and location by fitting a point-source model to seismic data from strong-motion and broadband stations. EPIC is designed for rapid magnitude determination in the early stages of rupture and is suitable for small to moderate events (M < ∼7). In contrast, FinDer (Finite Fault Detector) computes magnitude by mapping the evolving spatial distribution of peak ground motions (e.g., peak ground acceleration, PGA, or peak ground velocity, PGV) and comparing the motions to empirically derived templates of finite fault ruptures. While FinDer effectively avoids magnitude saturation, extending the magnitude range to M 9.0+ and capturing rupture growth, the method remains fundamentally empirical, relying on precomputed ground motion patterns rather than physical modeling of the source.

 To address these limitations, GNSS-based approaches have emerged as powerful alternatives. Real-time GNSS networks can measure both static and dynamic displacements with high temporal resolution (1–10 Hz) and without clipping, offering an effective means to capture permanent offsets in the near-field (Bock et al., 2000; Nikolaidis et al., 2001; Wright et al., 2012). GNSS-based peak ground displacements have been used to quickly estimate earthquake magnitude through empirical scaling relationships (Crowell et al., 2013; Melgar et al., 2015), with successful applications in early warning systems across various regions. These approaches, now integrated into platforms like G-FAST (Crowell et al., 2018) in the Western U.S and REGARD in Japan (Kawamoto et al., 2016), also provide real-time centroid moment tensor and fault slip estimates (e.g., Crowell et al., 2018; Melgar et al., 2012).

 Rapid magnitude estimation algorithms require a seismic trigger, and GNSS instruments cannot detect P-waves. To circumvent this problem, data from collocated accelerometer and GNSS stations are used to generate unclipped broadband displacement and velocity waveforms that capture dynamic, earthquake-induced shaking and static offsets (Bock et al., 2011; Smyth & Wu, 2007). These broadband displacements and velocities (hereafter referred to as seismogeodetic data) can be used to provide rapid seismogeodetic earthquake magnitude (*Mwg)* estimates for thrust earthquakes (Golriz et al., 2023) (hereafter referred to as Golriz2023), within  2–3 minutes of earthquake origin time, using the vertical component of seismogeodetic displacement as an approximate source time function. However, normal and strike-slip events remain challenging due to different wavefield characteristics and radiation patterns. Golriz2023 used collocated (GNSS and acceleration) stations and far-field P-wave energy generated by a double-couple point source in a homogeneous elastic medium without accounting for seismic wave radiation pattern effects on each source to site path. Instead, they used a constant representing the average P-wave radiation pattern over the focal sphere (Tsuboi et al., 1995). [Our magnitude notation should not be confused with the Das Magnitude scale *Mwg* introduced by (Das et al., 2019) to address the limitations of the conventional *Mw* for magnitudes below 7.5].

 Accounting for radiation patterns in real-time is complex and requires knowledge of earthquake source parameters (strike, dip, rake), which can be obtained through a near real-time CMT solution. Although several algorithms have been developed for this purpose (e.g., W-phase; G-FAST), limitations remain. Inadequate azimuthal coverage can lead to ambiguous or unstable solutions (Duputel et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2017). Epicentral and centroid misalignment can introduce significant errors in fault orientation estimation, affecting ground motion and tsunami predictions(Crowell et al., 2018). Earthquake source depth estimation biases of up to 10 km have been observed, impacting the accuracy of radiation pattern calculations. Additionally, real-time CMT inversions are sensitive to data dropouts and latency, though G-FAST is robust against moderate errors. Lastly, the inclusion of grid search techniques for improved fault plane geometry estimation increases computational demands and is not yet feasible in a real-time environment.

 In this study, we expand on the work of Golriz et al. (2023), based only on the far-field P- wave term in Equation 1 of Madariaga et al. (2019), by adding the far-field S-wave and the intermediate-field P-wave and S-wave terms. We also account for all radiation pattern terms and use the radial component of the horizontal motion instead of the vertical component. Our new approach improves our ability to obtain accurate *Mwg* estimates for normal and strike slip fault events known to produce tsunamis (e.g., the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point, Alaska and the *Mw* 7.5 Palu, Indonesia strike-slip earthquakes, and the *Mw* 8.2 Chiapas, Mexico and the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands, Alaska normal earthquakes) and also extends its application to local earthquake early warning. We apply our approach to seventeen earthquakes, including the eight thrust events, and the single normal event, from Golriz et al. (2023), six strike-slip events, and an additional normal event. We also use the available seismogeodetic and/or accelerometer data to estimate P-wave arrival times at locations where only GNSS data are available, thus enhancing the usable network of GNSS stations.

 Our study is motivated by the goal of developing an operational seismogeodesy-based warning system for local tsunamis and earthquakes. To demonstrate real-world applicability, we leverage the operational capabilities of the NOAA/NWS/Pacific Tsunami Warning Center (PTWC), which provides a model for rapid tsunami warnings. PTWC performs real-time data reception, earthquake detection, location, and magnitude estimation, partially within the Earthworm seismic network environment (Hirshorn et al., 2019; Johnson et al., 1995)

### **2.2 Data Analysis**

 We gathered collocated GNSS and accelerometer data recorded during nine earthquakes with magnitudes in the range of 7.0 < *Mw* < 8.2. Following the practice of Golriz2023, we define a collocation as a GNSS-accelerometer pair separated by no more than 5 km. Seven of these earthquakes were caused by strike-slip faulting in various tectonic environments: North American and Eurasian plate boundary (*Mw* 7.0 Ridgecrest, California and *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor- Cucapah, Mexico), Arabian and Eurasian plate boundary (*Mw* 7.8 Pazarcik and *Mw* 7.7 Elbistan, Turkey), Mendocino triple junction where the Pacific, North America, and Juan de Fuca/Gorda plates meet (*Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino, California), and the subduction zone interface between the Pacific and North America plates (*Mw* 7.6 and Mw 7.3 Sand Point, Alaska). The other two earthquakes are normal faulting events in the Pacific Basin, offshore Mexico (*Mw* 8.2, Chiapas) and Alaska (*Mw* 7.9, Rat Islands) (Table 1a). For the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands earthquake, focal mechanism solutions reported by the GCMT catalog cannot differentiate between rupture on a near-vertical fault or a shallowly dipping fault (Motazedian & Ma, 2022). Since the reported focal mechanism involves oblique normal faulting, we represent the radiation pattern for this event using a shallow dipping fault (see Table 1), which aligns with other studies (e.g., Miyazawa, 2015; Ye et al., 2014).

 We also test our expanded approach on the dataset of eight thrust earthquakes analyzed by Golriz et al. (2023) (Table 1b). All high-rate (1-5 Hz) GNSS data are processed using the precise point positioning (PPP) software PRIDE-PPP-AR (Geng et al., 2019). Accelerometer data sampled at 50-200 Hz is obtained from EarthScope [\(https://ds.iris.edu/ds/\)](https://ds.iris.edu/ds/). ### **3.2 Incorporating Stand-Alone GNSS Stations**

 Currently, accelerometers are collocated with only a small number of the total available GNSS stations, thus limiting the widespread applicability of the seismogeodetic approach. Furthermore, GNSS instruments alone are not sensitive enough to detect earthquake P-waves  and determine the width of the coseismic window (Golriz et al., 2021). We overcome these limitations by interpolating the P-wave first arrival times from nearby accelerometers(collocated or stand-alone). We use P-wave arrival times directly from a travel-time curve for those arrival times that fall far enough away from their predicted arrival times (a difference of more than 15 seconds). Once a coseismic window is determined from an accelerometer, its size can be interpolated to the stand-alone GNSS stations, providing additional information that helps constrain the earthquake magnitude and associated uncertainty, especially when the collocated station network is sparse.

 We interpolate the coseismic window computed at collocated stations and/or stations instrumented only with accelerometers to the stand-alone GNSS stations using Delaunay triangulation from four closest neighbors. We apply the coseismic interpolation from accelerometer-only stations for events with fewer than five collocated stations. If a window cannot be interpolated due to network sparsity, we use the coseismic window of the nearest neighbor within 10 km. For the accelerometers, we first integrate the data into velocities and apply a high-pass filter to ensure consistency with the computation of the coseismic window using seismogeodetic velocities. We then use our algorithm to compute *Mwg* over the interpolated window, expanding the usable network. We perform the interpolation with and without applying the radiation pattern corrections. However, for the stand-alone GNSS stations we focus on the results when we do not apply the radiation pattern corrections as we also do for the seismogeodetic (collocated) station analysis to mimic a real-time implementation. We find that in most cases 12 estimates from stand-alone stations remain within ±0.2 magnitude units of the collocated estimates, underscoring the robustness of the interpolation method. Supplementing the available network with stand-alone GNSS data nearly doubles the number of stations for the *Mw* 7.0 Ridgecrest and *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor-Cucapah earthquakes. [We do not have any stand-alone GNSS stations for the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Island earthquake.] We summarize our results in Table 3 (the results for the coseismic window interpolation with the radiation patterns applied are summarized in Table S2).

### **3.3 Performance on Thrust Earthquakes**

 We evaluate our new algorithm on the eight thrust earthquakes from Golriz et al. (2023) (Table 1b) and provide a summary comparison in Table 4. We find that the intermediate term is not required for optimal performance (Equation 8; Cint = 0) as its inclusion leads to systematic underestimations of *Mwg* for most events (Table S3). As seen in Figure 6 and Table 4, the algorithm's performance on thrust earthquakes is comparable to the results obtained by the Golriz2023 method, when radiation patterns (RP) are included. Without the RP corrections, we observe a significant and persistent underestimate of up to 0.4 magnitude units compared to Golriz2023 and the GCMT *Mw* estimates, particularly for the larger events (*Mw* 8.8 Maule and *Mw* 9.1 Tokachi-oki earthquakes).

 Interestingly, for the GCMT *Mw* 6.9 Kilauea, Hawaii earthquake, the two versions of the new method (with and without RP corrections) yield very similar *Mwg* estimates: 7.28 ± 0.14 with RP corrections and 7.18 ± 0.12 without them (Table 4). Both are closer to the higher *Mw* 7.2 estimates reported by Bai et al. (2018) and Lay et al. (2018), which have been linked to the inverse relationship between dip and seismic moment for shallow depth faults. We attribute the minimal difference in *Mwg* estimates to the configuration of the stations, which are approximately aligned along a single value of (the horizontal angle between the slip direction and the station) of 180° (Figure S2) and are sufficiently distant that the zenith angle is about 90∘ 395 . This station configuration results in the far-field S-wave RP coefficient /. 396 equal to 1 (Equation 4), similar to the assumption made when RP corrections are not applied. Thus, the variation in the estimates arises only from the far-field P-wave RP coefficient, AFP. However, because AFP 398 is less dominant than AFS 399 due to the higher P-wave velocity, it has a negligible effect on the far-field coefficient (Equation 7), resulting in the small difference between the *Mwg* estimates with and without RP corrections.

### **4 Discussion**

### **4.1 Performance Across Faulting Styles**

 We demonstrate the performance of our algorithm on a dataset of seventeen earthquakes comprising nine moderate to large magnitude strike-slip and normal fault earthquakes, as well as the eight thrust earthquake dataset from Golriz et al. (2023), representing diverse faulting mechanisms and tectonic settings. The improved algorithm provides an accurate seismogeodetic moment magnitude estimate within 0.2 units of the "true" GCMT reported magnitude for most strike-slip earthquakes, without radiation pattern corrections (Table 1). Our method is suitable for real-time implementation, even when relying on a limited number of collocated GNSS and accelerometer stations. Using the horizontal displacement component, along with the intermediate term of the double couple representation of the displacement field (Equation 1), proves essential for accurate magnitude estimation in strike-slip and normal faulting environments. The use of the intermediate term reduces the difference between *Mwg*  and *Mw* (Figure S3) for two strike-slip earthquakes (*Mw* 7.0, Ridgecrest, California, and the *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor-Cucapah, Mexico), where both network density and geometry are favorable. The radial component exhibits a stronger signal with a higher signal-to-noise ratio than the vertical  component analyzed by Golriz et al. (2023) and has proven to be more effective in these settings at longer distances, with significantly improved *Mwg* estimates.

 Our physics-based approach does not rely on statistical analysis of historical earthquakes, thus accounting for event specific characteristics. The empirical PGD-based magnitude estimates (e.g., Ruhl et al., 2018) are inconsistent in their performance when applied to events outside the dataset used to derive their scaling constants. For example, the empirical PGD-based magnitude generally underestimates the magnitude for thrust earthquakes (Golriz et al., 2023), largely underestimates the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point and the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands, Alaska earthquakes, and largely overestimates the *Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino, California earthquake. These three earthquakes are considered more complex and thus are expected to deviate from non-ergodic empirically based relationships that do not account for event specific source, path, and site effects.

 The accuracy of our *Mwg* approximations to moment magnitude vary as a function of station density and coverage. For events with high station coverage, such as the *Mw* 7.0 Ridgecrest and *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor-Cucapah earthquakes, our *Mwg* estimates (without radiation pattern corrections) are within 0.05 magnitude units of the GCMT *Mw* values, in contrast to the rest of the earthquake dataset, suggesting that station coverage plays a significant role in estimating *Mwg*. For example, despite having the same GCMT moment magnitude and similar depth as the Ridgecrest event (Table 1a), the offshore *Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino earthquake has sparse near-source and azimuthal seismogeodetic coverage and we overestimate its magnitude with (12 = 7.76 ± 0.70) and without (12 = 7.39 ± 0.29) radiation pattern correction (Figure 3). This magnitude overestimation, particularly in the case without radiation pattern corrections, can be attributed to the azimuthal station coverage of the event. Most stations are positioned approximately perpendicular to the fault slip direction, which is expected to receive less energy in the radial component of the horizontal motion used in this study (Equation 4). By assigning the radiation pattern coefficients a value of 1, as done in the case without radiation pattern corrections, rather than a value closer to zero that better reflects this station configuration, we overestimate the *Mwg* (Equations 6-8).

 For the *Mw* 7.8 Pazarcik and *Mw* 7.7 Elbistan earthquakes, we tested both 1 Hz and 5 Hz GNSS displacement data. Higher-rate sampling can improve resolution of dynamic ground motion, reduce aliasing (Smalley, 2009), and improve accuracy, particularly when integrated with high-frequency acceleration data, as in this study. In our limited test cases, *Mwg* estimates were roughly similar for both rates (Table 2). For Pazarcik, the *Mwg* estimates are 7.61 using 1 Hz and 7.68 using 5 Hz data (1 station); for Elbistan, the *Mwg* estimates are 7.49 ± 0.09 from 1 Hz data (2 stations) and 7.56 ± 0.08 from 5 Hz data (4 stations). While the 5 Hz results suggest slight improvements, the dataset is too limited to draw strong conclusions. However, our findings are consistent with a similar analysis performed on 1 and 5 Hz GNSS data from the 2019 Ridgecrest earthquake (Melgar et al., 2019). Additionally, 5 Hz data requiressignificantly more storage space and stresses the real-time data flow, which may impact its practical use. Further analysis using more stations and events is needed to evaluate the utility of 5 Hz data for real-time applications.

# **4.2 Sensitivity to Radiation Pattern Corrections**

 We find that correcting for radiation patterns improves our *Mwg* estimates of moment magnitude, especially for normal and thrust faulting events, where the *Mwg* deviates the most from *Mw* if radiation pattern corrections are omitted. However, in the Golriz2023 approach, radiation pattern corrections are not required for thrust earthquakes. In contrast, for normal- fault earthquakes (e.g., Chiapas and Rat Islands)**,** neglecting the radiation pattern leads to systematic underestimation or overestimation, indicating that its inclusion is indispensable for both approaches to such geometries.

 For strike-slip events, our results show that radiation pattern corrections have minimal influence on *Mwg*, suggesting that they can be omitted in real-time applications where source parameters are uncertain. This is further supported by our results for the 2019 Ridgecrest and 2010 El Mayor-Cucapah events, where the inclusion or exclusion of radiation pattern corrections yielded nearly identical *Mwg* values. However, there are two exceptions. Analysis of the *Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino earthquake yielded a significantly improved estimate of *Mwg* 7.39 ± 0.29 without radiation pattern corrections, compared to *Mwg* 7.76 ± 0.70 when using radiation pattern corrections. This might be a result of incorrectly reported source parameters, as this event is considered very complex and has not yet been studied in detail. Another deviation is the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point earthquake with a very low no-radiation pattern corrections estimate of 7.33 ± 0.04, compared to 7.63 ± 0.12 with radiation pattern corrections, which we attribute to the complexity of a two-stage rupture process (Wan et al., 2022). Despite having a strike-slip faulting mechanism, the moderate dip of the fault (49°; see Table 1) is not typical of strike-slip ruptures. In contrast, for the 2025 *Mw* 7.3 Sand Point earthquake, which occurred in nearly the same location and had a similar dip (57°), our algorithm performed well, yielding a seismogeodetic magnitude of 7.21 ± 0.16. This difference may be related to variations in station configuration between the two events (Figure 2) or to additional rupture complexity associated with the 2020 event.

### **4.3 Relative Performance When Including Stand-Alone GNSS Station**

 Stand-alone GNSS stations yield *Mwg* estimates generally in agreement with those from collocated sensors for strike-slip and normal earthquakes (Table 3; and Figures 4 and 5). Our use of Delaunay triangulation or nearest-neighbor interpolation helps overcome limitations in collocated station coverage for regions such as Turkey and Alaska. For example, for the *Mw* 7.8 Pazarcik earthquake, we added four stand-alone GNSS stations, which allowed us to quantify magnitude uncertainty. Similarly, for the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point earthquake, three additional stand- alone stations improved the magnitude estimate and yielded a significantly more reliable uncertainty range. For the *Mw* 7.0 Cape Mendocino earthquake, we analyzed sixteen stand-alone GNSS stations at epicentral distances < 200 km (all collocated stations were located at distances > 200 km). Interpolation was successful due to the regional network density. This closer-to-source stand-alone GNSS network improves the magnitude estimate from 7.39 ± 0.29 to 7.24 ± 0.20. In this case, incorporating accelerometers to compute and interpolate the coseismic windows to the stand-alone GNSS network added two additional stations (13 in total) and resulted in a slightly higher *Mwg* estimate of 7.30 ± 0.23 (Figure S4). This suggests that using coseismic  windows interpolated from accelerometers may be less critical in regions with sufficient seismogeodetic coverage, such as California.

 Since stand-alone GNSS sites are usually closer to the epicenter, they perform similarly to collocated seismogeodetic stations located farther away due to a high signal-to-noise ratio in displacement. However, when only far-field stand-alone GNSS data is available, seismogeodetic networks are preferable due to their higher signal quality and high signal-to-noise ratio compared to data from stand-alone GNSS sites. Additionally, the coseismic window is explicitly calculated from the seismogeodetic data, whereas for stand-alone GNSS stations, it must be interpolated, introducing additional uncertainty into the magnitude estimation process.

### **4.4 Real-time Implementation Consideration**

 We compare the median seismogeodetic moment magnitude (*Mwg*) evolution over time for all earthquakes analyzed in this study with the results obtained by Golriz2023 (Figure 7). In general, our new approach converges to a stable *Mwg* estimate within less than two hundred seconds after origin time, when we apply radiation pattern corrections (orange lines). This rapid convergence includes large events such as the *Mw* 9.1 Tohoku-oki, Japan, and the *Mw* 8.8 Maule, Chile earthquakes. However, real-time implementation of this algorithm remains challenging, as a rapid and stable centroid moment tensor solution and the derived source parameters are not typically available during the first few minutes after an earthquake. For strike-slip mechanism earthquakes, our *Mwg* estimates converge to the GCMT *Mw* even without radiation pattern corrections (green lines), making this algorithm suitable for real-time implementation. In contrast, for thrust and normal faults (Table 1), our new algorithm significantly underestimates *Mw* if we do not apply radiation pattern corrections.

 Golriz et al. (2023) used only vertical components, which exhibit higher noise relative to the horizontal components, and while the Golriz et al. (2023) approach performs well for thrust earthquakes, it largely overestimates the magnitudes of strike-slip and normal events below *Mw* 7.6 (Figure 1) because of the elevated vertical noise contamination.

 The comparisons in Figure 7 illustrate that the faulting mechanism should inform the choice of which method to use to rapidly estimate *Mwg* in a real-time operational setting, when no radiation patterns are considered. In thrust environments, the method presented in Golriz2023 is preferred as it does not require radiation pattern corrections. Similarly, our new method is preferred for strike-slip earthquakes where radiation corrections are not required. One way to identify thrust events is to determine if the epicenter is above an offshore subduction zone ( e.g., using Slab2; Hayes et al., 2018). If the epicenter is located above a subduction zone, we can assume that the hypocenter lies on the slab and that it is a thrust event. This assumption becomes more likely as the moment magnitude exceeds about *Mw* 8.

 Slab2 tends to closely match the geometry of thrust earthquakes reported in the GCMT catalog and can help distinguish between different faulting mechanisms (Tables 5, S4). Although Slab2 can be used to differentiate between normal and thrust ruptures, it is specifically designed to represent subduction zones and does not capture normal fault geometries (see Tables 5, S4, e.g., the *Mw* 8.2 Chiapas, Mexico, and *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands events). However, a deeper event (i.e., beneath the top of the slab) might indicate a normal fault mechanism that can also aid in selecting  the appropriate algorithm (e.g., the *Mw* 7.9 Rat Islands event). In such cases, the Golriz2023 method is preferable for normal events, despite an overestimation of about 0.3 magnitude units, as our new algorithm without radiation pattern corrections underestimates by a similar amount (Table 2).

 We have demonstrated that using Slab2 in our workflow can help determine the hypocenter depth, which is typically more challenging to identify in real-time. We have also shown that our *Mwg* estimate is insensitive to the difference between the reported and Slab2- inferred hypocentral depths, which are mostly within 10 km (Table 5), resulting in nearly identical *Mwg* estimates. The interpolation of coseismic windows to stand-alone GNSS stations expands the applicability of the seismogeodetic method beyond the limited set of collocated GNSS and seismic stations. We highlight the importance of using all available seismic and GNSS data in real- time applications to improve the robustness of *Mwg* estimates. However, complex rupture geometries (e.g., multi-fault or cascading ruptures) or more isolated regions with limited instrumentation may render such interpolations unreliable. A quantification of the interpolation error (e.g., bias in window start time) would be valuable in future implementations.

 This study also identifies multiple limitations that pose challenges for real-time implementation. We show that for most of the analyzed events, the number of stations is small (≤ 5), and the azimuthal coverage is limited. For example, for the *Mw* 7.8 Pazarcik and the *Mw* 7.7 Elbistan earthquakes in Turkey, we have only one and two collocated stations, respectively. Although we underestimate the magnitude of both events, we use *Mw* estimates reported by the  GCMT, which provides an upper bound estimate for the Elbistan earthquake, ranging from *Mw* 7.5 (USGS) to *Mw* 7.7. This shows that our results are within their uncertainties. However, rupture complexity related to the unusual source fault dip of 46° (Table 1) for a strike-slip earthquake might also affect our estimate. While using radiation patterns may remedy this problem, as demonstrated for the *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point earthquake, the approach requires source parameters (e.g., strike, dip, rake) that are not available in real-time. However, future developments may enable more rapid source parameter estimation. In the absence of such information, algorithms that rely on average behavior without radiation pattern corrections will struggle to account for event-specific source complexity. Thus, for events with good instrumental coverage and sufficient data, such as those strike-slip earthquakes in California (e.g., *Mw* 7.0 Ridgecrest and *Mw* 7.2 El Mayor-Cucapah), the seismogeodetic method performs well. Our study motivates the continued deployment of high-rate GNSS stations with real-time data transmission and collocation with accelerometers to enhance GNSS-based early warning systems and to mitigate seismic and tsunami hazards.

 Another limitation of our hybrid approach is the confusion of strike-slip earthquakes with a thrust mechanism on subduction interfaces; this will trigger the Golriz et al. (2023) method for thrust earthquakes in real-time implementation. This is the case for the *Mw* 7.3 and *Mw* 7.6 Sand Point earthquakes. While Golriz2023 overestimated *Mwg* for both events, the *Mw* 7.6 estimate (7.83 ± 0.12) was within a reasonable error of 0.25 magnitude units, whereas the *Mw* 7.3 event was substantially overestimated (*Mwg* = 7.97 ± 0.35) (Table 2). Such cases may complicate the real-time assessment of similar events. However, earthquakes bigger than ~*Mw* 8 above offshore subduction zones are most likely interplate subduction zone thrust events.

# **5 Conclusions**

 This study presents a hybrid approach to seismogeodetic magnitude (*Mwg*) estimation, which builds on the framework established by Golriz et al. (2023) for thrust earthquakes. We enhance the original method by adding terms for far-field S-waves, intermediate-field P- and S-waves, and source radiation. We also expand spatial coverage by interpolating P-wave arrival times and coseismic end times from collocated seismogeodetic and/or stand-alone accelerometers to stand-alone GNSS stations. This hybrid solution combines both intermediate-field and far-field formulations, each tailored to different physical mechanisms, resulting in more accurate *Mwg* estimates. The new approach performs particularly well for strike-slip earthquakes. Importantly, the method is suitable for real-time implementation. While the complete algorithm includes radiation pattern corrections for maximum accuracy, we also demonstrate that a simplified version offers a viable and efficient solution for operational use. When integrated with tectonic context information derived from Slab2, this implementation provides a viable and efficient solution in both local/regional and teleseismic earthquake and tsunami early warning systems. Including radiation pattern corrections improves our *Mwg* estimates for strike-slip, normal, and thrust mechanisms. These corrections can be applied as real-time source characterization capabilities evolve, ensuring that the seismogeodetic approach presented here is both forward compatible and operationally relevant.
